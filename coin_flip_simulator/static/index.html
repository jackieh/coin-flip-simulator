The bet

I. At $1,000, the stake is 100% of what you have.

Whether a bet is dangerous depends on its size relative to your wealth, not on its dollar edge. This flip puts $1,000 on the line. The growth-maximising stake for these odds, the Kelly fraction, is 2.38% of wealth, so this exact flip is sized right once you have $42K.

Someone with log utility accepts it once the stake falls under 4.76% of wealth, from about $21K. Above that fraction, betting repeatedly makes the typical outcome shrink even though every flip has a positive average.

Reading the plate. The straightedge runs from your wealth on the left scale (more wealth sits lower) to the stake on the right. Where it crosses the middle scale is the share of your wealth at risk. Drag either orange end, or focus one and use the arrow keys. Moving the stake scales the whole bet and keeps the odds.

II. The average and the typical player disagree.

The expected value is the average over every possible run. Here it is +$25 a flip. But you only collect that edge while you are still playing, and one bad streak ends the game.

So the mean (solid line) is carried by a minority who get lucky early and pull away. The median (dashed) is what half of all players beat. When the two diverge, the average describes almost nobody.

III. Where everyone ends up.

This is the exact distribution of final wealth after 500 flips. It's lopsided: a block of players who went broke, then a long right tail of survivors.

That tail is where the average lives. A distribution like this is called positively skewed, and for it, mean > median is the rule, not an accident.

IV. Going broke happens early.

Risk of ruin is the chance you hit the point where you can't cover the next loss and have to stop. Ruin is absorbing: once you're there you can't win your way back, so the chance only ever rises.

Most of it arrives in the first few dozen flips, while you're still close to the edge. If you survive that, the +$25 edge slowly carries you away from it. The flat line is the chance of going broke eventually if you never stop.

Each row is a different starting bankroll. Reading left to right, the risk accumulates as you play more flips. The contour lines mark where it passes 1%, 10%, 50% and 90%. The orange line is you.

Survival needs a cushion of many losses, not one. Ruin falls roughly exponentially with wealth: each extra ~$21K of bankroll cuts the forever-risk by a factor of e.

VI. Why a loss hurts more than a win helps.

A utility function turns wealth into how much that wealth is worth to you. If it curves downward (it's concave), each extra dollar matters a little less than the last. So losing $1,000 costs more satisfaction than winning $1,050 adds.

The family used here is CRRA, constant relative risk aversion: u(W) = W1−γ/(1−γ), and ln W at γ = 1. Its parameter γ is your relative risk aversion, meaning you dislike losing a given percentage of wealth equally at any wealth. Your absolute risk aversion, γ/W, fades as you get richer, which is why the same flip looks better from a bigger pile.

Reading the plate. Each curve is scaled to match a dollar-for-dollar line at your current wealth. The chord joins the two outcomes of one flip. Its height at the average outcome is your expected utility. Slide left until the curve reaches that height, and you have the sure amount you'd swap the flip for.

VII. What one flip is worth to you.

The certainty equivalent (CE) is the guaranteed cash that you'd find exactly as good as taking the flip. The gap between the average and the CE is the risk premium: what the uncertainty costs you.

A risk-neutral player (γ = 0) always values the flip at the average, +$25. With any risk aversion it's worth less than nothing to the poor and turns positive only past a break-even wealth, which doubles with each doubling of γ, roughly.

VIII. Stake the Kelly fraction and your money grows fastest.

If you bet a fixed fraction f of your wealth every time, the typical (median) result compounds at g(f) = p·ln(1 + b·f) + q·ln(1 − f) per flip, where b = win ÷ loss. The Kelly criterion picks the f that maximises g: f* = p − q/b.

Bet less and you grow more slowly but more smoothly. Bet more and growth falls, reaching zero at about twice Kelly, even though every single flip still has a positive average. Half-Kelly keeps three quarters of the growth with half the swings, which is why practitioners like it. A CRRA investor with risk aversion γ bets roughly Kelly ÷ γ.

How this is calculated

  • Going broke means your wealth falls below the loss amount, so you can't cover the next tails. You stop and keep whatever's left.
  • After n flips with h heads your wealth is start + win·h − loss·(n−h). There are only n + 1 possible states, so every probability here except the sample runs is exact, computed by dynamic programming, not estimated by simulation.
  • Going broke if you never stop uses the fact that e−R·W is a martingale, where R solves p·e−R·win + q·eR·loss = 1 (Lundberg's adjustment coefficient, from insurance ruin theory). That pins the long-run chance within a band that narrows the more flips are computed.
  • Utility and certainty equivalents use CRRA utility u(W) = W1−γ/(1−γ) (log at γ = 1). The Kelly fraction is p − q·loss/win; for this flip it's 2.38%.
